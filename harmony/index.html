Harmony chart

Every chord of a key on one chart. The scale runs across the top, one column per semitone, so a major third plainly spans more boxes than a minor one. Each row is the chord built on one note of the scale. Change the key signature with the key change combo and the whole chart follows; play a chord and its row lights up, or, if it comes from outside the key, it gets a row of its own with what it's doing there.

Reading the chart

The numerals count up the scale: capitals for major chords, small letters for minor, ° for diminished and + for augmented. In a major key the pattern is always the same: I, IV and V major, ii, iii and vi minor, vii° diminished, which is why a song's chords so often come from those seven. A chord outside the key is named for the job it's doing: a secondary dominant (V of vi) pulls toward another chord of the key; a borrowed chord comes from the parallel minor or major (♭VI from C minor in C major).

Scales of six or eight notes are counted 1 to 8 rather than in numerals. Barry Harris's sixth-diminished scales show why he built them: every other note gives the sixth chord and the diminished seventh by turns, each in all its inversions, so a melody moving through the scale is harmonized a chord a note. Play any diminished seventh and all four of its rows light, since B°7, D°7, F°7 and G♯°7 are the same four notes.
